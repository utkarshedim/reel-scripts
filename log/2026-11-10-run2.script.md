# Script: Ring Drone Cam (2026-11-10, run 2)

## 1. Research

Primary source: Amazon's own announcement, "Ring announces Ring Robotics" (aboutamazon.com, 10 Nov 2026), read in full. Cross-checked with Engadget (Cherlynn Low, 10 Nov), The Shortcut (Kevin Lee, 10 Nov, which interviewed founder Jamie Siminoff) and MobileSyrup (24 Sep 2020) for the earlier drone.

## 2. Zest and angle

**Zest:** Amazon's Ring made a $250 drone that flies around inside your house to check on alarms, and it's flown by the cloud over your Wi-Fi, not by a brain on board.

**Angle:** The "wait, what?" fact is that the drone barely thinks for itself. Ring's founder says the cloud tells it where to go, and if your Wi-Fi drops, it lands. Hook on the drone flying around your empty home, pay off with how it navigates (cloud, not on-board), keep alive a second question (why did it take years?), and end on the catch: a flying camera in your home, video through Ring's cloud, and it isn't even approved for sale yet.

## 4. Full script (about 110 seconds)

Amazon just showed a security camera that flies. A little drone that takes off from its dock and patrols your house while you're out. And the wild part? The drone barely has a brain. Something else is flying it.

It's the Ring Drone Cam, from Ring, the doorbell company Amazon owns. Two hundred and fifty dollars. It sits in a dock, camera off. When one of your Ring cameras or alarms goes off, it takes off and flies to the spots you've set, to see what's going on.

So who's flying it? The cloud. Ring's founder, Jamie Siminoff, put it simply: the cloud is telling it where to go. Over your Wi-Fi, an AI system called AI Pilot uses the drone's camera to map your home, plan the route, and steer it around your sofa and doors. The drone itself keeps just enough smarts to land. So if your Wi-Fi drops, it waits, and then it comes down.

Why? Siminoff says the idea goes back to 2016. An earlier version never really shipped, because it just didn't work well enough. A version with all the sensors and computer on board, he says, would have cost over five thousand dollars. Move the brain to the cloud, and the drone gets light. It weighs a hundred and sixty five grams.

Now the catch. It flies for under ten minutes per charge. It's loud, on purpose, so you know it's in the air. It's US only, it doesn't ship until January twenty twenty seven, and Amazon's own fine print says it isn't approved by the US regulator yet. And it's a flying camera inside your home, sending video through Ring's cloud. Amazon says the camera is off when docked and the video is encrypted. Whether you trust that is up to you.

So next time someone says AI is going into robots, remember this. This flying robot works because most of its brain isn't in the robot at all.

## 30 to 40 second version

Amazon's Ring just showed a security camera that flies. A two hundred and fifty dollar drone that patrols your house when an alarm goes off. And it barely has a brain. Ring's founder says the cloud tells it where to go. It streams video over your Wi-Fi, AI in Amazon's servers maps your home and steers it, and if your Wi-Fi drops, it lands. That's how they made it light, because a version with everything on board would have cost over five thousand dollars. The catch? Under ten minutes of flight, US only, ships in January, and it's still waiting for regulator approval.
